Intro to Computer Vision

Teaching machines to see — from pixels-as-numbers through classic models and MLPs to convolutional networks, augmentation, and transfer learning.

What you'll be able to do after this lesson

  • Represent images as numbers and prepare them for ML.
  • Classify images with classic ML and fully connected networks (CIFAR10).
  • Explain what makes a convolutional network convolutional.
  • Load and organize image data from Kaggle, Colab, or your own folders.
  • Train a cats-vs-dogs CNN and measure what augmentation adds.
  • Reuse pretrained models with transfer learning.
Reading Classic ML + MLP CNN Classifier Step-by-Step Guide
Before you start

Module 10 (neural nets, Keras, training discipline) is assumed — this module spends it on images. Theory: Computer Vision with Deep Learning PDF. Image datasets are heavy — the notebooks download CIFAR10 and Kaggle archives as they go, so run them somewhere with disk space.

1Images are numbers

Everything starts by seeing a picture the way a model does.

A color image is a 3-D array — height × width × three color channels — and every pixel is just a number. The module's running example is CIFAR10: 60,000 small color images in 10 classes, 6,000 per class. "Reading" an image means loading that array; "preparing" it means scaling pixel values and shaping batches the network accepts.

2First attempts: classic ML and MLP

Baselines before brilliance (Module 10's rule, reused).

The first notebook downloads and prepares CIFAR10, then tries two ladders: classic ML on flattened pixels, and a fully connected (MLP) network from Module 10's toolkit. Neither sees spatial structure — pixels are just long vectors — which is exactly why their ceiling motivates §3.

Why start weak on purpose?

A CNN that scores 80% means nothing until you know flattened pixels score far less. Baselines turn "it works" into "it works because of the convolutions."

3Convolutional networks

Layers that look at neighborhoods, not just lists.

Two theory lectures introduce the CNN (slides in the module PDF), and the CNN notebook makes it concrete: a first application, then a full cats-vs-dogs classifier trained on real photos. Convolutional layers learn local patterns (edges → textures → parts) wherever they appear in the frame — the spatial structure §2 threw away.

MLP (§2)

Every pixel connects to every neuron — position means nothing, parameters explode, translation breaks it.

CNN (§3)

Small filters slide across the image, reusing weights everywhere — fewer parameters, translation-tolerant, hierarchy for free.

4Reading and preparing images

The unglamorous half, three ways.

Three videos cover the same workflow on three platforms — Kaggle, Colab, and your own machine — all following the step-by-step notebook: import, unzip the training archive, visualize samples, create organized directories, and stream images with a generator that rescales on the fly:

image-classification-for-beginners-step-by-step.ipynb
from tensorflow.keras.preprocessing.image import ImageDataGenerator datagen = ImageDataGenerator(rescale=1./255) # pixels 0-255 → 0-1 train = datagen.flow_from_directory(...) # folders = labels
Folders are labels

flow_from_directory reads the class straight from the folder name — train/cats/ vs train/dogs/. Organize directories once, and labeling handles itself.

5Augmentation and transfer learning

Two answers to "we don't have enough images."

5.1 Data augmentation

Artificially multiply the training set with modified copies — flips, rotations, zooms applied on the fly during training. The CNN notebook trains with and without it, so the effect of augmentation is measured, not preached: same model, same data, the gap is the technique's value. Bigger variants add layers, batch normalization, and dropout (Module 10's §4, reused).

5.2 Transfer learning

High-accuracy training needs massive data (think ImageNet scale) — so reuse knowledge learned elsewhere: take a pretrained model and apply it to your related task, from direct prediction down to feature extraction. The step-by-step notebook closes on exactly this, with concrete pretrained examples.

The practitioner's order

Baseline (§2) → plain CNN (§3) → augmented CNN (§5.1) → pretrained transfer (§5.2). Each step answers "was the last one the bottleneck?" — stop climbing when the score stops moving.

◐Wrap-up — vision in one breath

The module compresses into a single pipeline.

the whole story
pixel arrays --> organize + rescale --> baseline (MLP) --> CNN --> augment --> transfer --> labels
The one-sentence version

Computer vision is Module 10's networks pointed at pixel arrays: prepare and rescale the images, baseline with classic models, let convolutions learn spatial structure, multiply data with augmentation, and borrow pretrained knowledge when your own dataset runs out.
